Jillian, Emma, and I went to Eden Dock straight after the Thames Barrier, which is about as different a place as you could pick. It sits in the middle of Canary Wharf, in the strip of water between the towers, right outside the tube station. You come up the stairs and walk straight into it.

There has been water here for a long time. A board on the quayside runs the whole story, from a Bronze Age wetland that people crossed on wooden trackways, through a medieval chapel and a wall built to keep the tidal Thames off the grazing land, to seven windmills grinding grain in the 1600s. The dock itself opened as the Export Dock in 1806. It cost £197,738 to build, about £9 million in today's money, and took 21 million bricks. The docks closed at the start of the 1980s. It went by Middle Dock from 1987 until 2024, when Canary Wharf Group and the Eden Project reopened it as Eden Dock, with planted pontoons floating on the water and a boardwalk running along the edge.

What we did not expect were the people. There are twenty-one life-size figures standing in the planting and out on the islands, handmade from sphagnum moss and dried flowers by a topiary firm called Agrumi. The piece is called Nature Rising. The figures form a trail that leads you from Jubilee Park down to the water. You keep catching another one out of the corner of your eye.

Eden Dock was worth the stop. The real treat, though, came afterwards. We walked over to B. Bakery nearby and had croissants, which were the highlight of the afternoon for all three of us.
